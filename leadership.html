Technology Built for Main Street

Background

“How much should a website cost?” Big agencies are currently charging, a few thousand dollars in building costs, a monthly fee, and additional add-on fees so you can get everything your company needs to succeed. I want to change this.

At Palmetto Advisors Group in Columbia, South Carolina, I help small businesses get custom websites and software that fit how they run. Twisted Whisk, a bakery in Lexington, SC had an outdated Wix site. After meeting with them, we revamped their website and have already driven 34 orders through their site in the last month. This allows them to focus on baking instead of IT and is just one success story. After having conversations with multiple business owners around South Carolina, I started noticing that owners aren’t against technology in fact, they know they need it. The issue is that most technology is overpriced and sold with big companies in mind. Small businesses are then left with two options: go without it or overpay for something that was never built for them.

This is the problem I want to work on. Not because I think technology companies are evil, but because I think the businesses that make up our communities deserve technology that truly serves their needs, at a price that makes sense.

Importance of the Issue

So, you may be asking, “Is this really a problem, or is it just something you’ve seen at work?” Fair question. Let’s look at the numbers.

First, who are we talking about? In South Carolina, there are 530,402 small businesses. That is 99.4% of all businesses in the state. They employ 863,326 people (42.9% of the state’s workforce), and between March 2023 and March 2024 they created 70.9% of the state’s net new jobs (SBA Office of Advocacy, 2025). In South Carolina, small businesses aren’t a niche market, they are almost the entire economy.

Second, are they actually behind? Yes. About one in three small business owners don’t have a business website, and more than half say they need technology training (Small Business Majority, 2024). The gap gets even clearer with newer technology. According to the U.S. Census Bureau (2026), AI use among firms with 250 or more employees has climbed to 37%, while fewer than 20% of firms with four or fewer employees use AI. In other words, the gap between big and small isn’t closing. It’s growing.

Third, why does this matter? Because technology isn’t just a “nice to have.” Deloitte found that digitally advanced small businesses earn about 2x the revenue per employee, see nearly 4x the revenue growth, and are almost 3x as likely to create jobs compared to small businesses with only basic digital tools (Deloitte, 2018). When a small business falls behind on technology, it isn’t only the owner who loses. It’s the employees, the customers, and the community.

Finally, when small businesses do adopt technology, it often comes at a steep price. Amazon now takes more than 50% of the average seller’s revenue in fees, up from about 40% five years earlier (Marketplace Pulse, 2023). U.S. merchants paid $187.2 billion in card processing fees in 2024, an 8.7% increase in a single year (Nilson Report, 2025). Big companies have the leverage to negotiate these costs down. Small businesses usually just pay them.

Goal and Initiative

My goal is simple: help close the technology gap for South Carolina small businesses by delivering technology that fits the business.

SMART Goal: By April 30, 2027, through Palmetto Advisors Group, I will deliver 30 websites or software tools to South Carolina small businesses at an average cost of $60/month with each site built in 3 hours or less using a reusable, AI-assisted build pipeline, and with every owner trained to manage their site on their own.

This initiative has three parts:

  • Build it cheaper. Use reusable components and an AI-assisted build pipeline to cut build time from days to hours. Less time spent building means a lower price for the client.
  • Sell what they need, not what costs the most. Use a needs-first sales process so every recommendation is based on the client’s actual pain points.
  • Leave them confident. Train every owner to manage their own technology, so they aren’t dependent on us (or anyone else) for every small change.

Steps to Implement

Step 1: Listen first (November 2026). Before building anything, I will survey 25 small business owners in the Columbia area about their current technology, what they pay for it, and what stops them from adopting more. This is the “Ask” phase of C.A.S.H.U. applied to an entire market instead of one client (Key Insight 2).

Step 2: Build the reusable pipeline (November – December 2026). I will finish our library of reusable components and the AI-assisted build pipeline, with a target of 1–3 hours per site. Write it once, reuse it everywhere. Every hour saved here is money saved for the client (Key Insight 1).

Step 3: Build the sales process and train partners (December 2026 – January 2027). I will turn our sales process into a documented playbook and help onboard 5 South Carolina sales partners. Each partner will practice through roleplay before their first real conversation (Key Insights 2 and 3).

Step 4: Discover, build, and deliver (January – April 2027). For each client, we start with discovery: What does your business actually need? What are you paying for now? What isn’t working? Only then do we recommend and build a solution using the pipeline from Step 2 (Key Insights 1 and 2).

Step 5: Hand it off with confidence (every project). Every project ends with a hands-on training session where the owner practices updating their own site. My goal isn’t to create customers who depend on us. It is to create owners who are confident with their own technology (Key Insight 3).

Closing

Big companies have entire IT departments. Small businesses usually have the owner, a phone, and not enough time to focus on software. I want to make sure that’s enough. By combining what I’ve learned about building software, selling with purpose, and building confidence through practice, I believe I can help South Carolina’s small businesses get technology that truly serves them. Technology should fit the business, not the other way around.
